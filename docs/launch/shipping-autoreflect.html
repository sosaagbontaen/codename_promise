Read this one first

Your first tenet stops at the edge of the device

Never lose what the user said or wrote. The code honors that beautifully — explicit flushes, durable audio before transcription, resumable sync, a migration suite that now opens real stores. All of it protects against process death, interrupted syncs, and restore-from-backup.

None of it protects against a lost phone. There is no account, no cloud copy, no export. A user who drops their iPhone in a lake loses everything, and they will have chosen your app specifically because you promised they wouldn't.

That gap is invisible from inside the codebase, because the code is correct. It is glaring from outside it. Whatever else you do, do not ship a durability-first journal with no way to get the journal back.

More of the hard part is done than you probably think. This is not flattery — most first apps ship without any of the following, and it is the reason the gap list below is as short as it is.

01

What you are actually selling

This is upstream of every other decision in this document. You cannot write an App Store subtitle, shoot a screenshot, or choose a community to post in without it — and it is the thing that nearly made you kill the project, so it gets its own phase.

The lesson from the silly apps is not the one it looks like

A height-predictor app clearing six figures a month does not prove that value is irrelevant. It proves something narrower and more useful: legibility beats sophistication. That app answers one question, in three words, that a specific person is already anxious about and already typing into a search box.

You have the inverse problem. The engineering is genuinely unusual — adversarial durability tests, a word guard that stops the model rewriting you, a migration suite that opens real stores — and none of it is legible in three seconds. Steal the legibility, not the emptiness. Building something you find contemptible is a bad plan even when it works, because you will not have the stamina to keep shipping it.

The invisible-benefit trap

"Never lose your work" is the truest thing about this app and the weakest thing to lead with. Every competitor implicitly claims it, a stranger cannot verify it from a listing, and a user only feels it on the day it saves them — which may be never. It is a reason to stay, not a reason to try.

Lead with something a person can see happening.

The claim that is actually differentiated

Not storage — assembly. The fastest path from "something happened" to "it is written down, on the right day, with the photos."

Two features you already have or nearly have make that literal rather than aspirational: importing a week of photos and routing each to the day it was taken, and showing which days are still blank. Nobody else does the first one. Together they are a fifteen-second demo, which is exactly what a screenshot and a listing need.

The second claim, and the one that earns the trust: it structures what you said without rewriting it. Anyone who has pasted their own writing into a chatbot has felt it come back flattened. That is a remembered, specific annoyance, and you are the only journaling app that enforces the fix rather than promising it.

Why it is hard to name, and the fix

The journal does not live in this app. It lives in Notion. That makes AutoReflect a thing that sits in front of another product, and products in that position are always harder to name than products that simply are a thing — "a notes app" needs no explanation, "the place your notes start before they go to your notes app" does.

This is a solved problem, and the precedent is worth studying: Drafts occupies exactly this position for text, and named itself out of the corner with four words — Where Text Starts. It does not claim to be your writing app. It claims to be the doorway. That framing built a real business in a category people said was already full.

So the category is a capture app: fast, reliable, forgiving, and pointed at somewhere else. Say the destination out loud, because naming Notion is what makes a stranger understand you in one sentence instead of three.

Say it like this

Four registers, same product. Pick by who is asking.

And the paste-able one, for a model or a doc that needs the mechanics:

AutoReflect is an iOS journaling app that acts as a capture layer in front of Notion. You speak or type an entry; the audio is saved to disk before transcription is even attempted, so nothing is ever lost. It structures your text into a bullet hierarchy without changing your words — enforced by a word-level diff, not just a prompt. Photos and videos are routed to the day they were taken. It shows which days in a range still have no entry, checked against both the phone and the Notion database. Finished entries sync to Notion as their own page, resumably and idempotently. The journal lives in Notion; the app is the fast, reliable front door to it.

Knowing whether it landed

Say the one-liner and listen for which question comes back.

"How does it know which day the photos belong to?" — you have them. That is the assembly claim doing its job, and the demo follows naturally.

"Oh, like Day One?" — you have lost them, and the fix is to lead harder on Notion. Day One is a destination; you are a doorway. Anyone who hears "journaling app" without hearing "into Notion" files you next to fifty apps you are not competing with.

"People who journal" is not an audience you can reach. "People who already journal most days, prefer talking to typing, and keep their life in Notion" is a person you can find in three specific places this week.

Small and high-intent beats large and vague. People who have journaled daily for years are unusual, passionate, and easy to identify — and they are the only ones who will feel the durability work at all.

Pick it and let it govern the roadmap. Everything that shortens "something happened" to "it is filed properly" is the product; everything else is a feature you happen to have.

It also tells you what to demo, and demo-ability is the whole listing.

Being "the thing that gets notes into X" is a commodity position: you are one roadmap item away from X shipping it themselves, and a second integration doubles the OAuth, block-model, chunking and idempotency work you already know the cost of.

Markdown-plus-media export answers "I do not use Notion" for most people, and it is already the top blocker in this document for a completely different reason. One weekend closes the biggest hole in the product and the biggest objection to it at the same time. Add a second deep integration when users tell you which one, with their wallets.

What it is and who it is for. It goes on the landing page, in the App Store subtitle, and at the top of every post. If you cannot write it, you do not yet know what you are selling — and that is a product problem wearing a marketing costume.

Find twenty people who journal regularly and ask what breaks in their current setup. Do not pitch. If "I take photos all week and cannot remember which day was which" comes back unprompted, you have your listing written for you. If it never comes up, you have learned that cheaply and can aim somewhere else while it is still cheap.

This is a real product for a real niche. Whether that niche is a business is genuinely unknown, and anyone who tells you otherwise is guessing. Decide now what would count — a hundred people who use it weekly, a thousand, a full-time income — because "it did not become GoTall" is not a verdict on anything.

02

Decide the shape before you build anything

Almost every item further down changes depending on this one choice, so make it first. Your backend today is explicitly single-tenant — one Notion token in one JSON file, one shared Groq key, an in-memory idempotency store. That is honest for one person and cannot serve strangers. You have three ways out.

ShapeWhat it meansCost to youTrade
A · No backendRecommended for 1.0 On-device transcription and formatting. Notion OAuth via a thin stateless proxy that holds nothing. Zero per-user cost. No servers to run, breach, or scale. AI quality below Groq. Real engineering to swap the providers.
B · Bring your own key Each user pastes their own Groq key; you never touch inference. Near zero. No inference bill, no shared quota. Brutal onboarding. Caps you at a few hundred technical users.
C · Full SaaS Multi-tenant backend, accounts, subscriptions, you pay for inference. Months of work, ongoing ops, real liability. The only shape that becomes a business. Also the only one that can bankrupt you on a bad month.

My honest read: ship A. Your product thesis is local-first, and the most aligned version of it has no server at all. It removes multi-tenancy, inference cost, key theft, GDPR data-processor obligations, and uptime from your first release simultaneously. You can add C later once you know people want it — you cannot easily un-ship a server holding strangers' journals.

Concretely: Apple's on-device speech recognition handles dictation, and on newer devices Apple's on-device language model can do the structuring pass your wordguard already polices. Keep the guard — it becomes even more important when the model is smaller.

Blockers in the code

The lede above. Minimum viable version: CloudKit sync (you deliberately kept the schema compatible — ADR‑008b) plus a plain "export everything" that writes markdown and media to Files. The export alone is a weekend and closes the worst of it.

PRODUCT_NAME is $(TARGET_NAME), so the icon label is your codename. Set a display name, and decide whether the bundle id stays com.codenamepromise.journal — it is permanent once you ship and cannot be changed afterward.

No asset catalog exists in the target at all. App Store Connect rejects submissions without a 1024×1024 icon. This is a hard stop and easy to forget until the upload fails.

Only relevant if you keep a backend. CP_API_KEY is one shared secret; anyone can pull it out of the binary in minutes and spend your Groq quota. A shared secret in a distributed client is not authentication.

A date range with the days that have no entry, each tappable to start it. Cheap to build, and it does three jobs at once: it is the retention hook, it is half the assembly claim, and it is a screenshot.

Frame it as an invitation, not a report card. Streaks work for language learning because missing a day really is a failure. In a reflection app, guilt is the thing that makes people delete it. "Tuesday and Thursday are still open, and here are the photos from them" is warm; "you broke your 14-day streak" is why people stop journaling.

Not fun exactly — this is somebody's private life, and jokes and confetti curdle fast. Aim for warmth and responsiveness: haptics on commit, a save state that reassures rather than reports, real transitions, copy written by a person. The interior of an entry is where all the time is spent and it currently looks like a form.

One deliberate flourish beats twenty scattered ones. The moment worth spending it on is right after you stop talking and the words appear.

Right now a new user opens an empty list. They need to learn in about twenty seconds that capture is instant, that Notion is optional, and that their words never leave the device unless they say so. That last point is your differentiator — say it out loud.

You currently learn about bugs because you are the only user. Add crash reporting before strangers arrive, or you will be debugging from one-star reviews.

MARKETING_VERSION is 0.1. Pick a scheme now and automate the build number, because you will be uploading a lot of builds and App Store Connect rejects duplicates.

04

Legal and compliance

This is the part first-time founders skip and then scramble on during review. None of it is hard; all of it takes calendar time.

Every app needs one, no exceptions, and the URL goes in App Store Connect before you can submit. It must actually describe what you do — including, if you keep a backend, that journal text and audio are sent to a third-party inference provider.

Apple's privacy labels. Answer honestly and narrowly — "data not collected" is a genuine competitive advantage for a journal, and it is only available to you on path A. Getting this wrong is a compliance problem, not a marketing one.

Your OAuth integration is currently internal to your own workspace. Distributing to other people's workspaces means making it public, which requires a privacy policy URL, terms URL, and Notion's own review. Start this early — it is a queue you do not control.

You use HTTPS, so you are almost certainly in the exempt category, but you must still declare it. Set ITSAppUsesNonExemptEncryption in the plist so you are not answering the question on every single upload.

You can ship as an individual — the App Store then displays your legal name publicly, which some people mind. An LLC costs a few hundred dollars, puts a business name on the listing, and separates your personal assets from the product. For a journaling app holding sensitive personal data, that separation is worth more than usual.

Before you spend anything on branding, search the trademark register and the App Store. "AutoReflect" is a plausible-sounding name, which is exactly the kind that turns out to be taken. Finding out after launch is expensive.

On path C you become a data controller processing sensitive personal data, and Groq becomes your processor — which needs a data processing agreement and a real answer on retention. Read their terms before you promise anything in yours. On path A this row disappears entirely, which tells you something.

If you ever offer account creation, Apple requires deletion from inside the app — not an email to support. Another row that only exists on path C.

05

Security

You are already ahead here — tokens never touch the device, the API key lives in the Keychain, media is contained. What is left is mostly about the transition from one user to many.

SwiftData files inherit iOS file protection, which is meaningful but tied to the device passcode. For a product whose pitch is "your private journal," being able to state your encryption position clearly is part of the pitch. Decide it deliberately rather than inheriting a default.

A security contact and a stated response commitment. It costs nothing and is the difference between a researcher emailing you and a researcher tweeting.

Per-user rate limits so one account cannot drain your inference budget; an audit that journal text never reaches your logs; automated dependency alerts. Also run your own /security-review against the diff before each release.

06

Getting it in front of people

Is any of this actually necessary?

Two piles, and they get confused with each other constantly. One is required — you cannot ship without it, and it has nothing to do with marketing. The other is how anyone finds out, which is optional in the sense that you can skip it and launch to silence.

ThingReally necessary?Why
Landing page Yes — but not for marketing Apple needs a privacy policy URL to accept the submission. Notion needs a privacy policy and terms URL to approve a public integration. You need a page whether or not a single visitor ever reads it.
Beta testers Yes Not a marketing activity. You cannot see your own onboarding — you know where everything is. Five people who are not you will find things you are structurally blind to.
Outreach Optional, and it decides everything Skip it and you will get roughly zero installs. The App Store is a checkout counter, not a discovery engine. Nobody browses it for journaling apps.
Brand identity, logo system, ads No An icon and one consistent colour is a brand at this stage. Paid ads before you know who wants this is setting money on fire.

Positioning is the whole game

The journaling category is enormous and mostly undifferentiated. You are not competing on features — you are competing on one specific promise nobody else makes credibly, which is that the app will not lose what you said. You have the receipts: an adversarial test suite, a decision register, a migration suite that opens real stores.

Lead with the failure, not the feature. "Never lose a thought mid-sentence" beats "AI-powered journaling with Notion sync" in every channel, and it is the sentence that will make a certain kind of person immediately understand you built this for yourself.

If you take the no-backend path, your second sentence is "your journal never leaves your phone" — which is a marketing message and a compliance answer at the same time, and almost nobody in this category can say it truthfully.

Minimum viable version is one page: the sentence, three screenshots, a "notify me" email field, and links to privacy policy, terms, and a support email. A static page on a free host is fine. This is a Saturday, not a project.

It blocks both the Apple submission and the Notion integration review, so it is genuinely first — ahead of anything else in this phase. Framer is fine, so is a static page on a free host; the tool is not the hard part.

Put it up while review is pending rather than after. That waiting week is free audience-building time, and the waitlist is the only list you will have on launch day.

TestFlight internal testing needs no review and is nearly instant; external testing reaches up to 10,000 people but the first build needs a review pass, so start it earlier than feels necessary.

Watch a first run without helping. Say nothing, take notes, and count the seconds before they understand what to do. Every instinct will scream at you to explain — the moment you explain, you have destroyed the only data you were going to get.

Do not ask "do you like it". Ask what they expected to happen, where they got stuck, and whether they would use it tomorrow. "I like it" is politeness; "I did not know what to tap" is gold.

Screenshots are the entire conversion funnel — most people never read the description. Show the product doing the thing your positioning claims, with a caption on each. The subtitle carries almost as much search weight as the title, and the keyword field is invisible to users, so use it for terms you would never put in prose.

Not a growth strategy — three specific communities you are genuinely part of, joined weeks before launch so you are a member rather than a drive-by advert. For this product the honest shortlist is Notion communities, journaling communities, and the indie-dev crowd who will care about the engineering story.

Turning up on launch day to a community you have never posted in is the single most common way a first launch goes nowhere.

"I made a journaling app" is ignorable. "I journaled every day for six years, my tooling kept eating my dictation, so I built one that cannot" is a thing people forward to a friend.

You have unusually good material here: the founding bug, the AI that had to be stopped from rewriting your voice, a migration that nearly ate your own journal and the test suite that now prevents it. Most people launching an app have nothing this specific to say.

Launch is a habit, not an event. The single-day-big-bang model is how first-time founders end up demoralised: one post, a quiet afternoon, and the conclusion that nobody wants it.

Ship, tell one community, learn, improve, tell the next. Product Hunt and similar are worth doing after you have a few hundred people who like it, not as the opening move — they reward an audience you already have.

Canny is the polished option and it is paid. At this stage you do not need voting, you need a list: an in-app "send feedback" that opens a mail composer, and a public page you maintain by hand. Fider is open-source and self-hostable when you outgrow that, and several hosted boards have free tiers worth comparing once you actually have requests to sort.

Ten users do not need a voting system. Ten thousand do, and you will know when.

A welcome email and a launch announcement to the waitlist need nothing but a list — Loops and Resend both have free tiers for a small one.

"Seven days inactive" is a different animal. Emailing someone about their behaviour means you hold an account for them and track their usage server-side, which reintroduces the entire multi-tenant, GDPR-processor problem you avoided by having no backend. Use a local notification instead. It runs on the device, needs no account, knows nothing you have to protect, works offline — and for a journaling nudge it is simply better, because it can fire at the hour they usually write.

An icon, a type choice, and a colour you use consistently. You do not need a brand system. You need to not look like a weekend project in a grid of competitors.

Paid ads before you know who converts. Press releases — no journalist reads them. Buying followers or "influencer" outreach for a $3 app. A Discord server with no members. Rebuilding the landing page a fourth time because it is more comfortable than talking to people.

That last one is the real risk for an engineer. Polishing is work that feels productive and reaches nobody.

07

Money

On path C every minute of dictation costs you money and every formatting call costs you money. Measure your own usage for a month — minutes transcribed, formatting calls, tokens — and compute the cost of a heavy user, not an average one. Journalers are not average; the people who love this app most will use it daily.

A flat subscription against a variable cost is how indie apps quietly lose money on their best customers. On path A this problem does not exist, which is another argument for A.

A one-time purchase suits a local-first tool with no running costs and sets expectations honestly. A subscription is the only thing that funds ongoing inference. Free with a paid tier gets the most installs and the most support burden. Pick the one that matches the shape you chose in phase 01 — they are not independent decisions.

App Store Connect will not pay you until the paid apps agreement is signed and tax and banking details are complete. It is tedious, it involves forms, and it takes longer than you expect. Start it the week you decide to charge.

08

The release cycle you asked for

This is what turns "I could upload it today" into a practice you can sustain.

Internal testing needs no review and is nearly instant. External testing takes a review pass but lets you hand builds to real people. Every release goes through TestFlight first — including the ones you are sure about, especially the ones you are sure about.

You do need to know where people fall out of onboarding and whether anyone returns on day seven. PostHog has a free tier generous enough for this stage and can be self-hosted; check its current limits rather than trusting a number written here.

The trap: the moment you add analytics, "data not collected" stops being a truthful answer on Apple's privacy questionnaire — and that answer is one of the best things this app has to say. Weigh it deliberately. If you do add it, instrument events only: never entry text, never titles, never transcripts, never counts specific enough to fingerprint someone. A journaling app leaking content through its analytics is the one mistake there is no recovering from.

You have 150 Swift and 129 Python tests that run in under three seconds combined. That is a gift — wire them to run automatically so a red build stops a release rather than a user finding it. Your migration suite in particular should gate every single release.

Bump version, run both suites, migration test against a fixture from the previous shipped build, TestFlight, soak for a few days, submit, then tag the commit. Write it down. You will skip steps from memory at 1am and that is precisely when the schema breaks.

This one is specific to you and you have just lived through why. The moment a version ships, capture a store written by it and check it in. Without that, the next migration is untested in exactly the way the last one was.

Pick a rhythm you can hold — every two weeks is plenty. Use App Store phased release so a bad build reaches a fraction of users before everyone. Keep the ability to halt a rollout, and know how to use it before you need to.

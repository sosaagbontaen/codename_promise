An assessment of the app as built against the direction you have just described, and what it would take to get from one to the other. Measured against the code, not against my memory of writing it.

The headline

Your instinct is right, and the drift is real, but it is smaller than it feels. The Notion-shaped code is about 2,000 of roughly 15,500 lines, and almost none of it is load-bearing for what you want to build. The durability machinery underneath it, which is where most of the real work went, becomes more important under the new direction rather than less.

What is not smaller than it feels is one thing, and it is not a Notion problem at all.

The finding that changes the plan

The rule that makes the app safe is the rule that forbids the feature you want most

Tenet 3 is “AI assists, never authors”, and it is not a slogan. It is enforced by backend/app/wordguard.py, which compares formatted output against the source word by word and returns a 422 if anything was introduced or dropped. A word may only be replaced by one that is recognisably the same word, at a string similarity of 0.72 or better. That is what makes softwaaarre → software legal and ran → jogged illegal.

Now read what you asked for:

“Minute 1: I talk about work. Minute 4: a relationship. Minute 8: I remember something else about work … produce a coherent journal entry where related thoughts are grouped together.”

Doing that to a ten-minute ramble means dropping filler, cutting false starts, and moving minute 8 up next to minute 1. Every one of those is a rejection under the current guard. The formatter is a copy-editor by design, by prompt, and by enforcement. You are asking for an editor. This is the single biggest piece of work, and it is not UI.

The resolution, and why it keeps the promise

Do not weaken the guard. Split it.

  • rawText stays inviolate. Nothing may ever rewrite what you actually said, and the recording stays until its transcript is merged. That is ADR-002 and it does not move.
  • The organised entry becomes a derived document that is allowed to cut and reorder, guarded by a different and still-mechanical rule: every section must cite the transcript spans it came from, and every substantive sentence in the transcript must be cited by some section.

Coverage is checkable in code, exactly as word-identity is today. And the citations are not just a guard, they are the best feature in the design: tap any line in your finished entry and hear yourself say it. That is what makes an AI-organised journal trustworthy instead of unnerving, and no competitor built on plain summarisation can offer it, because they threw the mapping away.

What survives, and matters more now than it did

This is the reassuring half, and it is real rather than consolation. The expensive work was never the Notion integration.

  • The durability layer becomes the spine. Audio is durable before transcription is attempted and released only once merged (ADR-002). When voice was one input among four that was prudent. When voice is the product, it is the whole trust story: a ten-minute recording is now the most valuable object in the app.
  • Local-first store, versioned schema, migration tests. The thing that already broke twice on your own phone and now cannot.
  • Export and import. You said you want a home you can move data into and out of. That round trip exists, with 230 tests behind it, and it is now a headline feature rather than a safety net.
  • The gap-finder machinery. JournalGaps and JournalCompleteness already answer “which days are empty” and “which entries are half-finished”. Your #2 needs one new signal fed into the same machine.
  • The visual card and collage system, the design language, the whole capture surface. Your #3 is largely built.

The MVP, in your words

“I had a day. I open the app. I talk about it naturally. The app turns my messy thoughts into a beautiful, organized record of that day.”

Everything in that sentence exists today except the word organized. Press record, talk, stop, the audio is safe, it transcribes, you get an entry, you can browse it, you can take it with you. The missing piece is the transformation in the middle, and the MVP is that piece plus the trust mechanism that makes it safe to believe.

The architecture

The strongest recommendation in this document is about what not to build. You do not need a pipeline, a vector store, an agent, or a RAG layer to ship the core magic. The chain that exists is:

audio → transcript → copy‑edit → entry

The chain you need is:

audio → transcript → organise → entry

That is one new service call, one new prompt, one new guard, and one new field. The field goes on EntryDraft as a @Model attribute rather than inside EntryContent — adding it to the composite would flatten into new columns and break the store, which is ADR-008a and the bug that already cost two days.

Everything else is reuse: TranscriptionCoordinator already leases, retries and merges; FormattingCoordinator is 93 lines and already has the snapshot-and-discard shape; the raw/organised toggle already exists in the editor.

The plan

0Prove the organiser before building anything around ithalf a day

Do this before any code. Record yourself rambling for ten real minutes about a real day. Run the transcript through candidate prompts in a scratch script and read the output. No app, no UI, no schema.

This is the entire product risk in one place. If the organiser is not good, nothing else on this list matters; if it is good, everything else is mechanical. It is also the cheapest test you will ever run, and doing it first is the difference between a week of work and a month.

1The core magicthe MVP
  • Long-form recording that is comfortable for ten minutes: elapsed time, pause, and a visible guarantee that it is already saved.
  • The organiser endpoint, returning sections with transcript span citations.
  • The coverage guard, replacing word-identity on this path only.
  • The entry view: organised sections, with tap-to-reveal what you actually said.
  • One schema version bump, done by the existing rules.
2Continuity from your camera rollyour #2

Today the app can only import photos you pick. It never asks the library what you did on a day. Reading PHAsset counts by date turns “August 17 is empty” into “August 17: 23 photos, 4 videos”, and then into a prompt that opens with those photos as memory triggers.

Modest work on top of machinery that already exists, and it is the feature most likely to make someone open the app on a day they would otherwise skip.

3The archive worth openingyour #3

Mostly built. What is missing is the year-scale view: months of days as imagery rather than a scrolling list, so opening the app shows you your life rather than your filesystem.

4Talking to your journalnot yet

Deliberately last, and deliberately not designed for now. It needs a corpus that does not exist yet, and every architectural decision made in its name today would be made without evidence. The citation mapping built in phase 1 is the foundation it will want, so building phase 1 properly is preparing for this.

One thing I would push back on

You said not to let accuracy and liability concerns drive the architecture unnecessarily, and for phase 4 I agree completely. For phase 1 I would not, and the citation design is why I am comfortable with the rest.

An app that rewrites your day and shows you only its version is asking for a kind of trust no product has earned. The same app that shows you its version and lets you hear the sentence behind any line has earned it cheaply, in about a day of extra work, and gets a mechanical safety check for free. That is not caution slowing the product down. It is the feature that makes the product credible.
